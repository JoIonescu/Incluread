How Incluread changes the reading experience

Three things happen when a reader uses Incluread. They're not incidental — they're what every design decision is in service of.

🧠

Reduces cognitive load

Syllable breaks, bionic reading anchors, and word spacing reduce the mental effort of decoding — freeing the brain to focus on meaning rather than letter-by-letter recognition.

👁

Eliminates visual stress

Tinted backgrounds, adjustable letter spacing, and focus line overlays address visual crowding and tracking difficulty that cause fatigue in 40–50% of people with dyslexia.

💪

Builds reading confidence

AI-powered simplification, vocabulary assistance, and chapter summaries give readers the scaffolding to approach challenging texts without anxiety or avoidance.

Built on evidence

Every feature is grounded
in peer-reviewed science

We don't ship features because they're trendy. We ship them because the evidence supports them.

Letter & Word Spacing controls
Increased letter and word spacing significantly improves reading speed and accuracy in dyslexic readers.
Zorzi et al. (2012). Extra-large letter spacing improves reading in dyslexia. PNAS, 109(28), 11455–11459.
5 background colour themes
Tinted overlays and coloured backgrounds reduce visual stress symptoms in 40% of people with dyslexia.
Wilkins, A.J. (2003). Reading Through Colour. Wiley. / Singleton & Henderson (2007). Computerised screening for visual stress in reading.
Dyslexia-optimised font selection
Fonts designed for dyslexia (OpenDyslexic, Lexend, Atkinson) reduce letter confusion by increasing unique character shapes.
Rello & Baeza-Yates (2013). Good fonts for dyslexia. ACM ASSETS. / Benton et al. (2004). Atkinson Hyperlegible design principles.
Read + Audio bimodal mode
Simultaneous reading and listening (bimodal input) improves comprehension and word retention for poor readers.
Rogowsky et al. (2016). Matching learning style to instructional method. Journal of Educational Research, 109(1), 64–78.
Syllable breaking mode
Breaking words into syllables at the point of reading supports phonological decoding — the core deficit in dyslexia.
Shaywitz, S. (2003). Overcoming Dyslexia. Knopf. / Dehaene et al. (2010). How learning to read changes the cortical networks for vision and language. Science.
Max-width reading column
Line lengths of 45–75 characters reduce tracking errors and re-reading in readers with visual processing difficulties.
British Dyslexia Association (2018). Dyslexia Style Guide. / Schneps et al. (2013). E-readers are more effective than paper for some with dyslexia. PLOS ONE.
